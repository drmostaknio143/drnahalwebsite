## BLOG 3

**EN — Title:** `Diabetes and Your Eyes: How Often Should You Get Screened?`

**Meta description:** `A simple guide for diabetic patients on how often to get a retina exam, and why it matters even without symptoms.`

**Body:**

`If you have diabetes, your eyes are at risk even when your vision feels completely normal. Diabetic retinopathy — damage to the retina's blood vessels from high blood sugar — often develops silently for years before any symptoms appear. By the time vision changes, damage may already be advanced.`

`### Why Screening Matters More Than Symptoms`
`Diabetic retinopathy in its early stages usually causes no pain and no noticeable vision change. The only way to catch it early is a dilated retina exam — not a routine vision/glasses check, but a specific examination looking at the retina itself.`

`### Recommended Screening Schedule`
`- Type 1 diabetes: first screening within 5 years of diagnosis, then annually.`
`- Type 2 diabetes: first screening at the time of diagnosis (since it's often present for years before diagnosis), then annually.`
`- Pregnant women with diabetes: screening in the first trimester, with follow-up as advised, since pregnancy can accelerate retinopathy.`
`- If retinopathy is already found: follow-up frequency increases — sometimes every 3–6 months — based on severity.`

`### What Happens During the Exam`
`Drops are used to dilate the pupil, allowing a clear view of the entire retina. This takes slightly longer than a regular eye check and causes temporary light sensitivity and blurred near vision for a few hours afterward — arranging a ride home is a good idea if this is your first time.`

`### What Raises Your Risk`
`Longer duration of diabetes, poorly controlled blood sugar (high HbA1c), high blood pressure, high cholesterol, and pregnancy all increase risk and may shorten the recommended screening interval.`

`### If Retinopathy Is Found`
`Early-stage retinopathy is often managed with closer monitoring and better blood sugar control alone. More advanced stages may need anti-VEGF injections, laser treatment, or in severe cases, vitrectomy surgery — all far more effective when started early, before vision loss occurs.`

`### The Bottom Line`
`A yearly dilated retina exam is one of the simplest, highest-value things a person with diabetes can do to protect their vision — regardless of how their eyes feel.`

## BLOG 4

**EN — Title:** `When Should You Get a Newborn's Eyes Checked? An ROP Awareness Guide`

**Meta description:** `A parent's guide to Retinopathy of Prematurity (ROP) — which babies need screening, when, and why timing matters.`

**Body:**

`For parents of premature babies, eye screening might not be the first thing on your mind amid NICU visits and monitors — but for some newborns, it's one of the most time-critical checks of all. Retinopathy of Prematurity (ROP) can progress from mild to sight-threatening within weeks, which is why screening timing is so specific.`

`### What Is ROP?`
`ROP happens when the blood vessels in a premature baby's retina — which haven't finished developing at birth — grow abnormally. In mild cases, it often resolves on its own. In severe cases, it can cause retinal detachment and permanent blindness if not treated in time.`

`### Which Babies Need Screening`
`Current guidance generally recommends screening for babies born before 34 weeks of gestation, or with a birth weight under about 2000 grams — though your neonatologist or pediatrician will confirm based on your baby's specific situation, since some babies outside these numbers may still need screening.`

`### When Screening Starts`
`Typically within the first 3–4 weeks after birth, timed based on the baby's gestational age at birth rather than a fixed calendar date — this is something your medical team will calculate and schedule.`

`### What the Exam Involves`
`A pediatric ophthalmologist examines the retina using a special lens after dilating drops are given. It's done at the bedside in the NICU or as an outpatient visit once the baby is discharged, and takes only a few minutes — though it can be uncomfortable for the baby briefly.`

`### How Often Follow-Up Is Needed`
`This depends entirely on what's found at the first exam — some babies need weekly monitoring, others can wait 2–3 weeks between checks, and some are cleared entirely after one or two exams. Your ophthalmologist will set the exact schedule.`

`### Treatment, If Needed`
`Depending on severity: continued monitoring, laser treatment applied directly to the retina, anti-VEGF injections, or in advanced cases, surgery. The earlier treatment starts when needed, the better the outcome.`

`### The Bottom Line`
`If your baby was born early or at low birth weight, ask your neonatologist directly about ROP screening — don't assume it's automatically included. Timely screening is one of the few interventions in medicine where a few weeks genuinely makes the difference between normal vision and permanent vision loss.`
